Meshing calculators

First-cell height (external & pipe), inflation layers, grid-convergence (GCI) and quality rules of thumb. Every result is a correlation-based estimate for initial mesh sizing and requires confirmation after solving. Values depend on the solver, wall treatment and flow.

Verification vs. validation — what these tools do and don't do

Code verification
Confirming the governing equations are implemented correctly (method of manufactured solutions, order-of-accuracy tests). Not addressed by these calculators.
Solution verification
Estimating numerical error in a given solution — iterative error and discretization error. The GCI calculator is solution verification. It estimates sensitivity and uncertainty associated with spatial discretization under the assumptions of the method; it does not prove the answer is physically correct.
Validation
Comparing simulation results against suitable experimental, analytical or benchmark evidence for the intended application. None of these calculators perform validation.

Credible CFD requires all of: correct geometry; an appropriate mesh; appropriate physical models; correct boundary and initial conditions; suitable numerical methods; iterative and conservation checks; solution verification; validation against trustworthy evidence; and uncertainty reporting. A converged grid-independence plot alone does not establish any of the others. Iterative error should be small compared with the estimated discretization error before a GCI result is meaningful.

Scientific basis, assumptions & references

Each equation below is stated with its coefficient definitions, required SI units, assumptions and validity range in the relevant calculator. Users should confirm every correlation against the documentation for their own solver and mesher.

Grid convergence (solution verification)

  • Celik, I. B., Ghia, U., Roache, P. J., Freitas, C. J., Coleman, H., Raad, P. E. (2008). “Procedure for Estimation and Reporting of Uncertainty Due to Discretization in CFD Applications.” Journal of Fluids Engineering, 130(7). The three-grid apparent-order, Richardson-extrapolation and GCI procedure implemented here follows this paper (safety factor Fs=1.25).
  • Roache, P. J. (1994). “Perspective: A Method for Uniform Reporting of Grid Refinement Studies.” Journal of Fluids Engineering, 116(3).

External flat-plate skin friction (local)

  • Laminar (Blasius): Cf,x = 0.664·Rex−1/2. Turbulent (Prandtl–Schlichting 1/5-power): Cf,x ≈ 0.0592·Rex−1/5. Schlichting & Gersten, Boundary-Layer Theory; White, Viscous Fluid Flow. Average forms (1.328·ReL−1/2; 0.074·ReL−1/5) are offered as a separate, consistent mode.
  • Flat-plate transition Reynolds number is not universal (commonly cited ~1.5×10⁵–10⁷ depending on freestream turbulence, roughness, pressure gradient, heat transfer).

Internal pipe friction

  • Laminar Darcy: fD = 64/ReD. Turbulent smooth (Blasius): fD = 0.3164·ReD−1/4, valid ~4×10³ ≤ ReD ≲ 10⁵. Darcy vs. Fanning: fD = 4·fF; wall shear τw = fD·ρU²/8 = ½·fF·ρU². Transitional band ~2300–4000 carries large uncertainty. See Moody (1944); White, Fluid Mechanics; Munson et al., Fundamentals of Fluid Mechanics.

y⁺ and wall treatment

  • y⁺ = ρ·uτ·y/μ, uτ = √(τw/ρ). Wall-resolved (y⁺≈1) and wall-function (~30–300) ranges are model- and solver-dependent; consult your turbulence-model and solver documentation (e.g. ANSYS Fluent / CFX Theory & User Guides, OpenFOAM turbulence-model docs) rather than treating any single range as universal.

Mesh-quality metrics

  • Skewness, orthogonal quality and aspect-ratio definitions and thresholds are tool-specific. Values shown are educational rules of thumb; confirm against the official documentation for the solver and mesher in use (e.g. ANSYS Meshing / Fluent quality metric definitions).
